Hey everyone, welcome back to the channel. Today we're doing a deep dive into how solar panels actually turn sunlight into electricity, and by the end of this video you'll understand the whole chain, from a photon hitting a panel to lights turning on in your house.

Let's start with a bit of history. The photovoltaic effect was discovered way back in 1839 by a French physicist named Edmond Becquerel. He was just 19 years old at the time. Photovoltaic literally means "light electricity," and the effect describes what happens when light strikes a material and knocks electrons loose, causing an electric current to flow.

So how does this work inside a modern solar panel? Each panel is made up of many cells, and each cell is built from silicon, a semiconductor. The cell has two thin layers. The top layer is treated with phosphorus, which gives it extra electrons and a negative charge; engineers call this the n-type layer. The bottom layer is treated with boron, which leaves it short of electrons, creating positively charged "holes"; this is the p-type layer. Where these two layers meet, called the p-n junction, an electric field forms. That electric field is the key to the whole process, because it acts like a one-way street for electrons.

Here's what happens on a sunny day. Photons from sunlight slam into the silicon cell and knock electrons free from their atoms. The electric field at the p-n junction pushes those free electrons in one direction, and that flow of electrons is electrical current. But here's an important detail: the current produced by the cells is direct current, or DC. Your home, your lights, your appliances — they all run on alternating current, AC. So every solar installation includes an inverter, a box that converts the DC from the panels into AC that your house can use.

Now let's talk efficiency, because this is the number everyone asks about. Commercial solar panels today convert about 15 to 22 percent of the sunlight that hits them into usable electricity. The rest of the energy is lost, mostly as heat and reflection. Yes, that sounds low, but it's worth knowing that a typical roof gets far more sun than a house could ever use.

There are three main types of panels you'll see on the market. Monocrystalline panels are cut from a single crystal of silicon. They're the purest, the most efficient — around 20 to 22 percent — and also the most expensive. Polycrystalline panels are made by melting multiple crystals together. They're a bit less efficient, around 15 to 17 percent, but they cost less to manufacture. Thin-film panels are made by depositing extremely thin layers of material on glass, metal, or plastic. They're flexible and lightweight, but their efficiency is lower, somewhere around 10 to 13 percent, so you need more roof space for the same output.

Which brings me to a practical tip: if your roof space is limited, monocrystalline gives you the most power per square meter, so that's usually the right call.

What about lifespan and maintenance? Solar panels are extremely durable. Most panels last 25 to 30 years, and manufacturers typically guarantee that a panel will still produce at least 80 percent of its original output after 25 years. Because panels have no moving parts, there's almost nothing to break. Maintenance is basically just cleaning the glass, which you should do one to four times a year depending on how dusty your area is. And here's a satisfying number: a panel typically produces in one to four years all the energy that went into manufacturing it. After that, it's pure gain. That's what we mean by energy payback time. Also fun fact — the price of solar panels has fallen by more than 90 percent over the last decade.

Finally, let's cover how a system connects to your life. If you're grid-tied, your panels stay connected to the utility grid. During the day, any electricity you don't use yourself is sent back to the grid, and under a policy called net metering you get credited for it. At night you simply draw from the grid like normal. If you go off-grid, you're completely independent, which means you need batteries — usually lithium-ion — to store the excess energy from the day so you can use it at night or during an outage. That's also why some grid-tied homeowners add a battery as backup. One thing to remember: panels only produce electricity while sunlight is hitting them, so they generate nothing at night. That's exactly why storage or grid connection matters.
